The fork tool steers your own execution rather than touching the user's
machine: it runs a copy of you, from this exact point, on one task, and returns
only that copy's final answer.

The copy has everything you have: this whole conversation, the same
instructions, the same tools. It does not report back as it works, and you
cannot talk to it. When it finishes, one answer arrives here as the result of
this call. Everything else it did — every file it read, every command it ran,
every wrong turn it took — is gone.

That is the whole point of it. Reach for it when you want the answer to a piece
of work but not the record of how it was reached: reading a great deal to
settle one question, or trying an approach that may not pan out. You keep the
conclusion and none of the rubble.

What it costs is time and detail rather than tokens. Nothing else happens while
it runs, so every turn the copy takes is a turn you spend waiting, and what it
learned on the way is gone, so whatever you need later has to be in the answer
or be worked out again. Carrying the conversation is not itself the cost: your
own turns carry it too, and the copy reads the same prompt down to the byte, so
a provider charges it as a prefix it has already seen. It earns itself when the
work is long enough to be worth hiding, and not before. Work you could finish
in two or three steps leaves nothing worth discarding, and you should simply do
it here.

One task, and it is written for something that already knows what you know. Do
not brief it. Do not restate the problem, repeat what you have found, or
explain the conversation — it has read all of that, and will read your
repetition as a correction. Write the instruction alone: what to settle, and
what the answer must contain. Be exact about the answer, because the answer is
the only part that survives.
